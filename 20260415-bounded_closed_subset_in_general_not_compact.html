bounded closed subset in general not compact

Proposition

Let $X$ be a bounded closed subset. Then $X$ is in general not compact

Proof

easy

Let $X$ be an infinite set Then consider the metric

\begin{align*} d(x,y) = \begin{cases} 0 & \mbox{if } x = y \\ 1 & \mbox{else } \\ \end{cases} \end{align*}

which induces the discrete topology.

In particular, any compact subset of $X$ is finite, hence $X$ is not compact. But $X$ is closed and moreover bounded since $d(-,-) \leq 1$.

$L^p$

Let $p \in [1,\infty]$ and consider $B_1[0] = \{x \in l^p \vert \| x \|_p \leq 1\} = \overline{B_1(0)}$. Consider $e^n = (0,..,0,1,0...,)$ Then $e^n \in \overline{B_1(0)}$, but for $n \neq m$

\begin{align*} d_p(e^n,e^m) = \begin{cases} 2^{\frac{1}{p}} & \mbox{if } p < \infty \\ 1 & \mbox{if } p = \infty \\ \end{cases} \end{align*}

so, the sequence $e^n$ cannot have a convergent subsequence, as no subsequence can be Cauchy (cf. konvergente Folge als Cauchy-Folge).

Hence $\overline{B_1(0)}$ cannot be sequentially compact. As $l^p$ is a metric space, any sequentially compact space would be compact (cf. Kompaktheit und Folgenkompaktheit in metrischen Räumen).
