dual of l0 and l1 are isometrically isomorphic

Proposition

There exists an bijective, linear isometry $l_0^* \rightarrow (l^{\infty})^{*}$.

Proof

We define

\begin{align*} l_0^{*} \rightarrow& l^1 \\ f \mapsto& (f(e_n))_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \end{align*}

observe that this map is linear as it is linear in each component. So it remains to show that this map is an isometric embedding and surjective (and hence a bijection, as isometries are injective).

isometry

we define $y_n = \sum_{i=1}^n e_n$ observe that $\| f \| \geq \frac{f(y_n)}{\| y_n \|_{\infty}} = \frac{\sum_{l=1}^n f(e_l)}{1}$. Hence passing to limits $n \rightarrow \infty$ shows

\begin{align*} \| f \| \geq \frac{f(e_n)}{\| y_n \|_{\infty}} = \frac{\sum_{l=1}^\infty f(e_l)}{1} = \| (f(e_n))_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \|_1 \end{align*}

which in paritcular shows that $\| (f(e_n))_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \|_1 < \infty$ or $(f(e_n)) \in l^1$. Now it remains to show $\|(f(e_n))_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \|_{1} \geq \| f\|$. Let $(x_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} x_i e_i \in l_0 \setminus \{0\}$, then we need to show that $\frac{\| f(x_n) \|}{\| x_n \|} \leq \|(f(e_n))_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \|_{1}$ and then passing to $\mathrm{sup}$ shows the claim above. Here we use

\begin{align*} \|f(x_n) \|_1 =& \| f(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} x_i e_i) \|_1 \\ =& \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \vert x_i f(e_i) \vert \\ \leq& \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \mathrm{sup}_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \vert x_i \vert \cdot \vert f(e_i) \vert \\ =& \| (x_n) \|_{\infty} \cdot \| (f(e_n))_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \|_{1} \end{align*}

where we have used that $f$ is continuous (as it is bounded). In particular, the last line shows $\frac{\| f(x_n) \|}{\| x_n \|} \leq \|(f(e_n))_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \|_{1}$ as we wanted.

surjective

Let $(x_n) \in l^1$, then we define $f \in l_0^*$ by

\begin{align*} f: \mathrm{span}(e_n) \rightarrow& \mathbb{K} \\ e_n \mapsto& x_n \end{align*}

Observe that this $f$ is is bounded for $\|f \| \leq \mathrm{sup}(x_n) < \| x_n \|$

Then the bounded linear extension theorem shows that this function extends to a bounded linear function $\tilde{f}: l_0 \rightarrow \mathbb{K}$, since $e_n$ is a schauder basis, so in particular $\mathrm{span}(e_n)$ is dense in $l_0$. Moreover, by construction, this $\tilde{f}$ maps to $x_n$.
